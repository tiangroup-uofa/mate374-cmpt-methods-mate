FORMAT
Two hosts, conversational, about 8–10 minutes. Audience: third-year materials
engineering students who just finished three lectures on molecular dynamics (MD).
Tone: curious, light, accurate. No equations read aloud beyond one or two simple ones.
Do not invent quotes, dates, or numbers. Use only the facts listed below. If a
detail is not listed, keep it general.

MAIN LINE

1. Cold open: a question MD cannot answer
   - We can simulate atoms vibrating, but can we watch an alloy decide how to
     arrange its atoms at 800 K?
   - An atom vibrates about 10^13 times per second but swaps places with a
     neighbour only about once per second. For every atom in a million-atom
     sample to move, MD would need about 10^16 time steps. Large MD runs reach
     about 10^9. So MD just sits there.
   - Hook: people hit this same wall eighty years ago, with neutrons instead of atoms.

2. Los Alamos, 1946: too many possibilities
   - The question: in a fission device, do neutrons multiply or escape?
   - Each neutron has a chain of events: collide, scatter, cause fission, escape.
     The answer is an average over all possible histories. Far too many to count.
   - Same structure as our alloy: an average over far too many arrangements.

3. The gambler's shortcut
   - Stanislaw Ulam relaxed with solitaire and liked poker.
   - In 1946, recovering from an illness, he wondered: what is the chance a game
     of Canfield solitaire comes out? Counting all card arrangements was hopeless.
     Why not just play many games and count the wins?
   - That is the whole idea: replace an impossible count with random samples.

4. Why now? The computer
   - Statistical sampling was an old idea that had fallen out of use because it
     was too tedious by hand.
   - The ENIAC electronic computer changed that. Ulam talked to John von Neumann,
     who in March 1947 outlined a calculation that followed many individual
     neutron histories at random.
   - Nicholas Metropolis suggested the name "Monte Carlo", partly because Ulam
     had an uncle who borrowed money from relatives because he "just had to go
     to Monte Carlo." Metropolis and Ulam published "The Monte Carlo Method" in 1949.

5. People had the idea before, but no machine
   - Buffon's needle (18th century): drop needles on a lined floor, count the
     crossings, and you can estimate pi. Our students did this in Lecture 1.
   - Boltzmann explained thermodynamics through the statistics of enormous
     numbers of atoms; his statistical view was disputed in his lifetime.
   - Enrico Fermi used random sampling for neutron problems in Rome in the early
     1930s with a small mechanical adding machine and never published it.
   - Later he designed a hand-pushed "Monte Carlo trolley", the FERMIAC, that
     drew neutron paths on a scale drawing.
   - Punchline: random methods need huge numbers of samples. They are a
     computer-age method.

6. The trick we actually use: Metropolis, 1953
   - Random samples alone fail for materials: almost every random arrangement
     of atoms has a tiny chance of occurring at a given temperature.
   - Metropolis, Arianna and Marshall Rosenbluth, and Augusta and Edward Teller
     found a rule, run on the Los Alamos MANIAC computer: propose a small change,
     always accept it if the energy goes down, and accept it only sometimes if
     the energy goes up, more often when it is hotter.
   - This visits arrangements in proportion to how likely they are, using only
     energy differences.

7. Back to the alloy, and the catch
   - A Metropolis "swap" can exchange two distant atoms in one step, something
     the real alloy never does. That is why it reaches the settled arrangement
     MD cannot.
   - The catch: the steps are not time. It tells you where the alloy ends up,
     not how long it takes.
   - Teaser: why do A and B not simply mix at random? Next lecture.

FACT GUARDRAILS
- Keep weapons context to plain facts; no dramatization.
- Do not claim Ulam invented random sampling; he revived it for computers.
- Do not give an exact year for Buffon or details of Boltzmann's critics.
- Do not say Monte Carlo replaces MD; it answers different questions.
