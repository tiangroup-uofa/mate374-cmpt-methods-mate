   derivative) vs discrete data. This lecture handles discrete data.
4. "Numerical integration on discrete data: Newton-Cotes formulas":
   I ~ integral of P_n, weighted sum, table of degrees 0-3 placed side by side
   with a 4-row figure (Quarto columns 58% / 38%). Direct vs composite figure.

   (expanded sum, then the sum symbol), "midpoint needs values between data".
7. Trapezoid: derivation from Newton's form, caution callout midpoint vs
   trapezoid, composite trapezoid for uneven spacing, tip callout "baseline".
8. Simpson 1/3: Newton form c1, c2, c3, result only (term-by-term integration
   in a collapsed callout), comparison of points used, S = (2M + T)/3 as a
   forward pointer to L20, composite rule with the panel figure, requirements.
9. Simpson 3/8: result, composite, 1/3 + 3/8 for an odd N. In-class notebook
   comparing all five rules. "Why stop at degree 3?"
10. Python: slicing formula vs np.trapezoid on a stress-strain table (toughness),
    then a tool list: np.trapezoid, scipy.integrate.simpson, romb, quad.

Corrections to the transcript and sketches
------------------------------------------
- "Simpson with h about 0.01 already gives 1e-16" is too optimistic. For the
  integral of sin x from 0 to pi, Simpson 1/3 with h ~ 0.01 has an error of
  1.1e-10, and the round-off level is reached near h ~ 3e-4. The page states
  these numbers. For e^x on [0, 1] the round-off level is reached at N ~ 1500.
- "We are safe to work on much fewer points when integrating than when
  differentiating" is overstated. More points reduce the noise in an integral
  (sigma (b - a)/sqrt(N)) while they increase it in a derivative, but a coarse
  grid still has a truncation error (6 points of x^3: 1.6%, almost all
  truncation). L20 states the corrected version: refine until truncation is
  below the noise, and extra points never make the integral worse.
- "No need to interpolate to make data integrable" is kept, and confirmed:
  the integral of a fitted cubic (0.23% typical) is no better than the
  trapezoidal sum (0.25%), and Simpson is not better either (0.24%).
- Tai trivia, checked against the PubMed abstract (PMID 8137688):
  - The abstract describes dividing the area into "rectangles and triangles",
    validation against the graph-paper method, and states that "other formulas
    widely applied by researchers under- or overestimated" the area. It does
    not say that rectangles were the taught method, so the page says "other
    formulas in use".
  - Her profession is not given in the paper (affiliation: Obesity Research
    Center, St. Luke's-Roosevelt Hospital Center), so the page says "Mary Tai"
    and does not call her a nurse.
  - Citations: Crossref lists 406 (checked Oct 23, 2026), not about 100. The
    page says "cited hundreds of times".
  - "Invented 300 years ago": Newton-Cotes formulas date from around 1700, but
    the trapezoid idea is far older. Babylonian tablets (350-50 BCE) compute
    Jupiter's displacement as a trapezoid area under a velocity-time graph
    (Ossendrijver 2016, Science). This is added because it matches the opening
    velocity-to-distance example.
- "Order 0, 1, 2, 3" of the Newton-Cotes rules is called "degree n" of the
  interpolating polynomial on the pages, to keep it apart from the order of
  accuracy O(h^p). Delta x from the old slides is written h; N is the number
  of subintervals (n is the polynomial degree).
- The Fall 2025 L17 slide 9 implies the trapezoid is more accurate than the
  midpoint rule. For smooth integrands, the composite midpoint error is half
  the trapezoidal error (opposite sign); L20 shows this with numbers and a
  figure.
- Water at the example's velocities in a 20 mm pipe would be turbulent
  (Re ~ 2e4), so a parabolic profile would be wrong. The worked example uses
  quench oil (rho = 900 kg/m^3) and states only that it is viscous enough for
  laminar flow; no viscosity value is quoted.
- Closed Newton-Cotes rules have negative weights at degree 8 and at every
  degree from 10 upwards (checked with exact fractions). Degree 9 does not.
